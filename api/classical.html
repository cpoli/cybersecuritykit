cybersecuritykit.classical#

cybersecuritykit.classical hub · History · Examples · API reference

Classical ciphers and their cryptanalysis, from Caesar to Enigma.

Shift, polyalphabetic, digraphic and fractionating ciphers, Enigma and Lorenz, Shannon’s information theory, and the cryptanalysis that broke each of them.

Every public name below is re-exported by the subpackage: import it as csk.classical.<name>. The plotting helpers are the exception: import them explicitly from cybersecuritykit.classical.visualizers, which loads Matplotlib.

Wheatstone’s Playfair cipher (1854), the first practical digraph cipher.

Invented by Charles Wheatstone in 1854 and promoted by Lyon Playfair; see D. Kahn, The Codebreakers (1967), ch. 6. Encrypting pairs of letters hides single-letter frequencies, leaving only the much flatter digraph statistics. Those statistics are still enough for simulated annealing over key squares; see M. J. Cowan, “Breaking short Playfair ciphers with the simulated annealing algorithm”, Cryptologia 32(1), 2008, doi:10.1080/01611190701743658.

cybersecuritykit.classical.systems.playfair.break_playfair(ciphertext, seed=2026, restarts=5, steps=50000)[source]#

Search for the key square by simulated annealing on English bigrams.

Each anneal starts from a seeded random square. A move swaps two cells, or occasionally two rows or two columns; it is kept by the Metropolis rule at a temperature falling linearly from a fortieth of the text length, since score differences grow with the text, and the decryption is rescored in full after every move. The best square over all anneals wins.

Parameters:

  • ciphertext (str) – Text from playfair_encrypt(); several hundred letters or more. With the default moves, one anneal finds the square on about half of the 400-letter texts tried, so five restarts rarely all fail there.

  • seed (int, optional) – Seed for the starting squares and the moves.

  • restarts (int, optional) – Number of independent anneals, at least 1.

  • steps (int, optional) – Moves per anneal, at least 1.

Returns:

The best square, its plaintext and score, and the number of squares scored.

Return type:

cybersecuritykit.classical.core.base.PlayfairBreakResult

Raises:

ValueError – ciphertext is not whole Playfair pairs.

Examples

>>> from cybersecuritykit import classical
>>> c = classical.playfair_encrypt("the harbour was quiet in the morning", "lighthouse")
>>> len(classical.break_playfair(c, restarts=1, steps=50).plaintext)
30

Kasiski’s examination (1863) of repeated fragments in polyalphabetic ciphertext.

F. W. Kasiski, Die Geheimschriften und die Dechiffrir-Kunst (Berlin, 1863). Charles Babbage had found the same attack earlier but never published it. A fragment of plaintext that recurs at a multiple of the keyword length is enciphered identically, so the gaps between ciphertext repeats share the keyword length as a factor.

Friedman’s index of coincidence (1922) and the column attack on Vigenère.

W. F. Friedman, The Index of Coincidence and Its Applications in Cryptography, Riverbank Publication No. 22 (Geneva, IL, 1922). Two letters drawn from English text agree about 6.6% of the time, from random letters only 1/26 = 3.8%; the columns of a Vigenère ciphertext taken at the right period are shift ciphers and recover the English rate.

The German ADFGVX field cipher (1918), broken by Georges Painvin.

Introduced on the Western Front in March 1918 (ADFGX, then ADFGVX from June); see D. Kahn, The Codebreakers (1967), ch. 9. A 6x6 Polybius square splits each letter or digit into two coordinates (fractionation), and a keyed columnar transposition then separates the halves.

cybersecuritykit.classical.systems.homophonic.solve_homophonic(symbols, seed=2026, restarts=4, steps=50000)[source]#

Assign letters to symbols by simulated annealing, then a greedy polish.

The score of a candidate plaintext with bigram counts \(n_{xy}\) out of \(N\) is \(\sum n_{xy} \log(P_{xy} / (n_{xy}/N))\), minus the Kullback-Leibler divergence of its bigrams from English (times \(N\)). Scoring conditional bigram probabilities alone rewards collapsing every symbol onto T, H and E; the divergence does not. Each move gives one random symbol a random letter and is kept by the Metropolis rule at a temperature falling linearly to zero; a final sweep then takes every single-symbol change that helps. Only the bigrams touching the changed symbol are rescored.
